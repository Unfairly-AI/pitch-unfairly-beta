# Copy that sounds like a person wrote it

People recognize machine-written copy within a slide or two, and once they do they stop believing the numbers next to it. Our first testers named the tells: sentence fragments, subtitles, and em dashes. This guide covers those and the patterns that travel with them. It applies to every word on a slide, the speaker notes, and the hand-over message.

The test for any line is to say it out loud to the person across the table. If you wouldn't say it that way, rewrite it.

## The hard rules

The design review checks every slide for each of them and blocks the deck until they're fixed.

1. **No em dashes.** Not in headlines, body lines, captions, notes, or the chat. An en dash or a double hyphen standing in for one counts too. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or parentheses. For a range, write "2019 to 2024" or use a plain hyphen ("9-5"). The only exception is a real quote that has one; mark its element `data-copy-ok`.
2. **Write whole sentences.** A headline or body line has a subject and a verb. Stacked fragments are the most common tell: "One link. Every screen." becomes "One link works on every screen." "Faster. Smarter. Simpler." becomes the one claim you can prove ("Reports that took a day now take ten minutes."). Labels aren't copy: chips, axis labels, button text, table headers, and mono eyebrows can be short phrases.
3. **No subtitles.** A headline stands alone. Don't put a line under it that restates it, softens it, or teases the next slide, and don't write "Title: subtitle" headlines ("Fizz Soda: The Future of Soda"). If a line under the headline earns its place, it adds a fact the headline doesn't have: who it's for, a number, a date, a source. The cover is the same: the promise, then the company name or the date, not a tagline under a tagline.
4. **No formula contrasts.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Not just X, but Y." "X isn't a feature. It's a movement." "Less X, more Y." State the claim directly.
5. **Plain words.** These read as generated wherever they appear: unlock, unleash, seamless, effortless, elevate, empower, supercharge, revolutionize, game-changer, cutting-edge, next-generation, harness, leverage (as a verb), robust, delve, landscape, journey, synergy, reimagine, transform, world-class, best-in-class, "in today's fast-paced world". Say what the thing does.

## The quieter tells

- **Rhythm by threes.** Lists of three adjectives or three short clauses ("fast, simple, and secure") are there for cadence, not meaning. Keep a list of three only when there really are three things, and you'd name them the same way out loud.
- **Colon reveals and answered questions.** "The result: 3x growth." "The catch? There isn't one." Just say it: "Revenue tripled."
- **Vague intensity.** "Dramatically faster," "massive opportunity," "truly unique." Swap the adverb for the number or the name: "Onboarding went from nine days to two."
- **Every line the same length and shape.** People vary their sentences. A deck where every headline is a five-word imperative reads as a template.
- **Middle-dot separators.** "Illustrative workflow · setmeld.com" or "Fast · Secure · Simple" strings fragments together. Write a sentence or cut the line.
- **Exclamation marks and emoji.** At most one exclamation mark in a deck. Emoji only when the brand itself uses them.
- **Title Case Headlines.** Write headlines in sentence case unless the brand's own site uses title case.

## Sound like the company

- Use the words the company uses. `deck_context` returns its voice, its lessons ("we say GTM engineer, never growth agent"), and earlier decks; their phrasing beats yours.
- Use contractions the way people talk: "we're," "it's," "don't."
- Be specific. Name the customer, the city, the number, the month. A specific line sounds written by someone who was there; a general one sounds generated.
- Keep the joke rare. One light line in a deck reads as personality; a pun on every slide reads as a template.

## Checking it

Read the headlines in order, out loud. Then read each slide's body copy and its speaker notes. Fix every em dash, every stacked fragment, and every line under a headline that repeats it. If a slide still sounds like an ad, cut words until it sounds like you explaining it.
